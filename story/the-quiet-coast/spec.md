## Production status and scope

This replaces the uploaded review draft. It preserves all 22 recorded Part 1 narration lines. Parts 2 and 3 are rewritten. Part 1 needs visual and sound revisions; its existing render cannot be certified without watching it. No audio, source project, or rendered video was supplied for this review, so all timings below are an editorial plan, not measured voice timings. This is ready to take into implementation and an animatic, not a claim that a final render has passed QA.

The story promises two answers: who is signalling from inland, and why did the sirens arrive so late? It answers both before the final moral reversal. Suspense shifts from survival in Part 1, to the stranger in Part 2, to the warning failure in Part 3. Do not promise that every detail stays unexplained until the last second: the audience needs time to understand the answer and feel the ending.

## 2. World, geography, and physical continuity

This is a fictional coast with a fictional local coast-watch network. The warning-office failure is a plot choice, not a claim about an actual agency or disaster.

Canonical coast-to-inland order:

1. Sea and harbor, lowest ground.
2. Bakery and town, on the coastal plain.
3. Lower hill shoulder and damaged broadcast mast.
4. Elevated mast maintenance landing and continuously rising ridge trail.
5. Forested ridge and emergency radio shelter, higher than the water reached in the shots.

The shelter belongs to the same coast-watch station as the mast but is a different structure at a higher elevation. Mara was operating there when the quake hit. Show the mast, the ascending trail, and the ridge lamp together in a Part 1 wide shot so this relationship exists before the escape needs it.

In lateral travel shots, the sea is screen-left and inland movement is screen-right. Looking backward over a shoulder is allowed; reversing Ravi's actual escape direction is not. Establish the relationship again before any new camera axis. Do not require an interior close-up to contain a literal sea on the left.

Ravi has a pale bakery apron over a dark shirt, a single torn apron corner after the impact, and a scraped right sleeve. Those details persist. Wet clothing, mud, exhaustion, and damage never reset between shots. Mara has a dark raincoat and carries the portable signal lamp. No unexplained dry costume change.

The lower mast is an improvised refuge Ravi reaches in an emergency, not a recommended evacuation destination. He never descends to the flooded coastal plain. The maintenance landing and upward path must be plainly visible before he uses them.

### Disaster rules

- Strong shaking is already a warning to leave the coast; silence from animals is atmosphere, not a reliable detection system.
- Depict a turbulent, debris-laden surge for this story. A tsunami can resemble a fast-rising flood or a wall of turbulent water; do not declare every wall-like appearance impossible. Avoid a clean curling surfing wave.
- Sea retreat is possible, not obligatory. The visible retreat in Part 2 is the lull between this story's first and next major surges.
- In this event the next surge reaches higher on the slope. Do not turn that observation into “the second wave is always larger.”
- Water slows as it runs up the incline. It does not halt because it meets trees. Flood lower trunks and the gully while the upper trail stays above the depicted run-up.
- A temporary lull is not an all-clear. The final scene keeps the characters on high ground; additional dangerous surges remain possible.
- Remove unexplained steam, storm lighting, and fire. The weather stays overcast; light progresses from night through dawn to early morning.

Reference basis: NOAA, “Tsunamis,” https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/ocean-coasts/tsunamis and “Tsunami Inundation,” https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/tsunamis/tsunami-inundation (checked 22 October 2026). These support the hazard behavior only. They do not validate invented terrain, mast strength, or the fictional incident timeline.

## 8. Remotion implementation contract

The following is a specification for the developer. No Remotion source, assets, schema, or render has been built or tested in this review.

### Compositions and timing

- Deliver three 1080 × 1920 compositions at 30 fps. Editorial target: 1,950 frames each, about 65 seconds. A compilation is approximately 195 seconds before any separate inter-part cards.
- Treat every time range in the tables as half-open: start included, end excluded. Scene duration is end minus start. At 30 fps, the boundaries are integer frames.
- Place recorded dialogue first; derive actual word and sentence cue points from the recording. The scene windows are provisional until the voice edit exists. Do not manufacture “verified” millisecond cues from text.
- If Part 1's locked audio differs from 65 seconds, set that composition to its real edited duration and conform its visuals. Do not change it merely to make all three files identical in duration.
- Keep narration off the final roughly one second where practicable, so each reaction or payoff can land. Fit P3's radio exchange with a real scratch read before recording the final voice; shorten surrounding narration if it crowds the ending.
- Use roughly 150–170 words per minute during spoken passages, with deliberate gaps for looks, danger, and the SOS cycle. Speech rate is a starting direction, not a substitute for measuring the recording.

### Scene data and validation

Each scene needs: stable `sceneId`, composition-relative `fromFrame`, `durationInFrames`, `lineIds`, `locationId`, `timeOfDay`, `cameraId`, `characterStartState`, `characterEndState`, `waterState`, `signalState`, `audioCueIds`, and `transition`.

Use explicit states, not one overloaded “mood” enum. Examples: `timeOfDay: dawn`, `weather: overcast`, `emotion: alarmed`, `locationId: ridge_path`. “Dusk-grey” must not masquerade as dawn.

Validation should reject duplicate IDs, zero/negative scene durations, accidental timeline gaps, missing line IDs, escape movement toward sea, disappearing injury/costume states, an intact mast after its brace fails, or an inland shelter silently renamed as the lower tower. Overlapping dialogue must be an intentional exception, not an accidental collision.

Store the lamp cycle's phase and character/world positions independently of shot boundaries. A new React scene mount must not reset a wave front, footfall, radio state, or lamp cycle. Reuse the exact end-state of P1 as P2's starting state, excluding only release-specific title overlays.

### Full SOS animation

Use one deterministic repeating distress cycle for all lamps: three short pulses, three long pulses, three short pulses. Use a short unit of 6 frames; a long pulse is 18 frames. Between pulses within a letter use 6 frames dark; between S and O, and O and S, use 18 frames dark; between full cycles use 42 frames dark. One full cycle including its repeat gap is 204 frames, or 6.8 seconds at 30 fps.

Both groups of three short pulses must visibly complete the pattern. Do not cut after the long pulses and call it SOS. Carry the phase across parts when the light is continuous. Include at least one complete readable cycle in P1 S7 and P3 S5. The answering lamps need not be phase-synchronized; each is operated by a different survivor.

### Animation and camera

- Use a consistent simple 2D stickman language: readable silhouettes, expressive poses, restrained paper texture, stable line thickness, and a limited night/dawn palette. Disaster scale comes from staging and sound as much as moving debris.
- Body motion needs planted feet, weight transfer, contact with ladder rungs, hands wrapping the rail, recoil at impact, and a tired recovery. A sliding character over a panning background is insufficient for this story.
- Animate in world coordinates, then apply camera motion. Tie the advancing surge to terrain elevation and a shared water-level state; never restart it at the left edge of each shot.
- Keep the harbor overview as a look from Ravi's established location; do not render a second full-size Ravi standing at sea level during those views.
- Seed procedural debris and particles. Render frame-by-frame reproducibly; do not use unseeded randomness or elapsed browser time as animation clocks.
- Use motivated cuts for a look, insert, or reaction and continuous motion across adjacent travel shots. A dissolve is optional for the explicit time-lapse. Dissolving every change of place would blur escape direction and consume suspense time.
- No whole-screen shake on every sentence, repeated white flashes, constant zoom pulses, or overlapping effects that obscure a crucial clue.

### Sound and text

- The quake rumble, physical window hum, radio static, delayed sirens, structural creak, surge roar, and dry bootstep must be distinguishable. Avoid literal total silence while water is still rushing below.
- Make the recorded desk reply audibly distinct from narration and visibly linked to the recorder. The live desk voice uses the same performer and voice character, then the live RX indicator establishes that it is a new call.
- Duck music and environmental sound around the office instruction, “Ravi?”, and the final request/reply. Test intelligibility on a phone speaker.
- Caption narration and dialogue from actual audio cues, generally one or two short lines. Keep the radio instruction and identity intact; do not split them into unreadable word flashes.
- Use a conservative working safe region of approximately x=90–900 and y=220–1500 for indispensable text at 1080 × 1920. Check against the actual destination app's current interface; these margins are not a universal platform guarantee.
- An important clue cannot rely on a tiny prop in a wide shot. Enlarge or frame it cleanly and hold it long enough to read on a phone. Avoid introducing the explanation in captions before its visual reveal.
